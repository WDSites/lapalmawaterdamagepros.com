How it works

Drying a building is a physics problem

Water leaves a wet material by evaporation. Moisture moves from the surface to the air, and then the air must be dried or replaced so evaporation continues. If the room air is already full of moisture, nothing dries, no matter how many fans run.

Structural drying controls three things at once: air movement, air temperature and humidity. The goal is to pull water out of framing, subfloor and drywall until each material matches a dry reference reading. The first 24 to 72 hours of response are covered on the water mitigation page, and this page explains the method and the measuring.

Temperature, relative humidity and grains per pound

Technicians track three numbers. Temperature tells how much moisture the air can hold, relative humidity shows how full the air is as a percentage, and grains per pound measures the actual weight of water vapor in the air.

Grains per pound matters because relative humidity swings with temperature. A warm room can show a lower percentage while holding the same moisture. A drying job compares readings inside the wet area with readings in a dry room and outdoors, and the gap tells whether equipment is winning.

An LGR unit runs best when the room is warm and closed. Windows stay shut during drying, which surprises people who expect fresh air to help.

Getting at moisture that hides in walls and floors

Wall cavities

Small holes are drilled at the base of a wall or the baseboard is removed so dry air can travel between studs.

Subfloor and slab edges

Thin boards and sheet panels are dried from above and below. Concrete holds water for days and needs patience.

Insulation

Wet batts and blown insulation hold water against the drywall and are often removed instead of dried.

Hardwood

A drying mat can pull water out of the boards, but cupped flooring may still need replacement.

Setting a drying goal and checking it each day

A drying goal is a target moisture reading for each type of material, set from a dry sample elsewhere in the same building. Moisture meters and a thermal camera check every wet spot daily and the results go on a log.

The equipment is adjusted as readings come in. Fans are moved, dehumidifiers are added or removed and wet pockets are opened. When every point meets its goal, the equipment goes out the door, and not before.

Safety steps on a drying job

Power draw

Dehumidifiers and air movers use real electricity. The crew spreads loads across circuits so a breaker does not trip while you are away.

Cords and trip hazards

Cords run along walls and stay out of standing water. Keep children and pets clear of the equipment.

Noise

Expect a steady hum for several days. Quiet bedroom hours can be discussed with the crew before equipment is set.

Dust and old materials

Opening old walls can release dust. Tell the crew if your home may contain older materials and ask them before cutting.

Why a household with more people dries differently

According to the U.S. Census Bureau, La Palma has 5,110 households with about 3.01 persons per household. More people means more showers, cooking and breathing, and each adds moisture to indoor air.

During drying, we ask the household to avoid long hot showers, indoor clothes drying and unvented cooking in the wet area. Those habits can add a measurable amount of moisture and slow the work.

Why the water source affects drying

Supply water in La Palma comes from imported Metropolitan Water District water plus groundwater from the Orange County Water District, which is recharged in part by the Santa Ana River. A leak from a clean supply line is category 1 water, so drying can begin without removal for contamination.

Gray or black water changes that. Porous materials may have to come out before drying starts. The category is defined in the published ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, and you can ask any company how it applies the standard to your job.

When drying fails and what comes next

When drying stalls, the first step is to find out why. We open one more section, move equipment or call for a plumber if the leak is not stopped. If rooms stay wet past the safe window, the mold remediation page explains what follows.

  • Readings stall above the goal for two days in a row
  • A musty smell returns after the equipment leaves
  • A hidden source, such as a slow supply leak, keeps adding water
  • Drywall or flooring cupped or swelled before drying started
  • Humidity outside the wet area is higher than inside it

What affects the number of drying days and the cost

Price follows the water category, the area wet, the number of dehumidifiers and air movers, the days on site and the monitoring visits. Dense materials such as concrete and hardwood take longer than drywall.

We explain scope and price before work starts. The real number comes from seeing the damage and taking readings, so call the 24/7 line to arrange it. Sudden and accidental damage is often covered by insurance and gradual leaks often are not, so check your policy and ask your insurer.

Mistakes to avoid while the equipment runs

  • Turning dehumidifiers off at night to save power
  • Opening windows in humid weather
  • Moving air movers to a spot that looks drier
  • Covering wet floors with rugs or plastic
  • Rebuilding before the final readings

Common questions

Can I call about structural drying at night or on a weekend in La Palma, CA?

Yes. The 24/7 line is open at night, on weekends and on holidays. The crew sets up equipment when a visit is arranged.

How long does structural drying take?

Often several days, but it depends on the materials, humidity and how deep the water went. Daily readings decide when it is done, not the calendar.

Why do you keep the windows closed?

Dehumidifiers work best in a closed, warm room. Open windows bring in outdoor humidity and make the equipment work harder.

Will my electric bill go up?

Yes, likely. Dehumidifiers and air movers draw real power over several days. Ask your insurer whether the added electricity is covered.

What is the difference between an LGR and a desiccant dehumidifier?

An LGR chills air to condense water and suits warm rooms. A desiccant unit uses a drying wheel and works in cooler spaces or when a very low humidity is needed.

How do you know the walls are dry?

A moisture meter reads each wall against a dry reference from the same material. The log shows every reading, so you can see the trend.
